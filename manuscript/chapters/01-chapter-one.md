The first chapter starts here. For now these pages hold a few notes on how the book works, set the way your own words will be: as printed pages you can turn, with a title page before them and a place for everything that comes after.

Nothing here is meant to last. When you have an opening of your own, ask for it to go in this chapter, or say what the chapter should do and ask for a first draft to react to. Either way, this text makes room for yours.

Each chapter is a plain text file. A blank line starts a new paragraph, a word between single asterisks is set in *italics*, and one between double asterisks is set in **bold**. That is nearly all the formatting a book needs. The page takes care of the rest: the indents, the margins, the line that carries the chapter's name across the top.

You do not have to write in order. Some people start with the scene they can see most clearly and work outward from it. Others plan every chapter in the outline before they write a sentence. The Desk keeps count either way, and shows which pieces are still an idea, which are drafted, and which you have called final.

When you do not know something yet, do not stop to look it up. Leave a placeholder such as [TK: the town's name] and keep going. The Desk counts every placeholder still open, so nothing is lost, and you can fill them in on a day set aside for checking facts.

A row of three asterisks on a line by itself makes a break like the one above. Use it where time passes or the point of view moves, and the page will mark the pause for you.

> A longer quotation, a letter, or a passage from another book can be set apart like this. Begin the line with an angle bracket and it sits a little in from the margin.

The Notes page is the book's memory. The outline lives there, with a line or two for each chapter, and so does the style sheet: how names are spelled, how dates are written, what the narrator would never say. Add to it the first time a choice comes up. A hundred pages later, when the same choice comes up again, the answer will be waiting.

Read your pages here as a reader would. A sentence that looked fine in a draft can look different once it is set in type, with a real margin around it and a page number in the corner. If a paragraph runs long, you will see it. If a chapter ends too soon, you will see that too.

Give yourself a number to aim for if that helps. The Desk shows the words you have against the length you set, and a short chapter written is worth more than a long one planned. Most books are made in small sittings: a page before work, a scene on a quiet evening, a week when it all comes at once and a month when it does not.

Keep what you cut. A paragraph that does not belong in chapter two may be the opening of chapter nine, and a line you loved and removed is easier to let go when you know where it went. A file in the notes is a good place for these, and nobody reads it but you.

Change only what you mean to change. Ask for a single paragraph to be tightened and the rest of the chapter stays as it was. Ask for the whole chapter to be redone and it will be, but your earlier version is kept, and you can go back to it.

Read it aloud now and then. The ear catches what the eye forgives: a word used twice in one line, a sentence that runs out of breath, a conversation in which everyone sounds the same. If you stumble while reading, a reader will stumble there too.

Let someone else read it when you are ready, and not before. A first reader does not need to be an expert. They only need to tell you where they were lost, where they were bored, and where they could not stop.

When the book is ready to leave the desk, it can be made into an ebook or into a manuscript for an editor, and the pages you see here can be printed just as they are. Until then there is only the next sentence. Turn the page, and begin.
